TITLE: The TSP Withdrawal Mistake That Costs Retirees Thousands Every Year

Quick TSP withdrawal explainer for Tumblr — shareable, reblog-friendly, useful for retirees and near-retirees planning their Thrift Savings Plan income.

The biggest TSP withdrawal mistake is treating withdrawals like an ATM instead of a strategy.

Most retirees either withdraw too much too soon (and risk running out of money) or too little too late (and miss out on income they could have had). Both problems usually come from skipping the planning step.

THE 4 PERCENT RULE, IN ONE LINE

The 4 percent rule says: withdraw 4 percent of your balance in the first year of retirement, then adjust that dollar amount for inflation every year after.

For a $300,000 balance, that is $1,000 per month. For a $500,000 balance, it is $1,667 per month. For a $750,000 balance, it is $2,500 per month.

But the 4 percent rule was designed for a generic retiree with no other income. Most TSP retirees have other income (military pension, Social Security, VA disability, part-time work), which means they may be able to withdraw more safely, or alternatively may want to withdraw less to avoid pushing themselves into a higher tax bracket.

THE TAX TRAP

TSP withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income. If you withdraw $3,000 per month from a traditional TSP balance, you are adding $36,000 per year to your taxable income. Depending on your other income, that can push you into a higher tax bracket and increase your Medicare premiums.

Roth TSP withdrawals are tax-free, which makes them especially valuable in retirement.

A TSP withdrawal calculator factors in federal tax owed on the withdrawal, so you can see your actual monthly take-home rather than just the gross amount. That makes a huge difference when you are planning your retirement budget.
