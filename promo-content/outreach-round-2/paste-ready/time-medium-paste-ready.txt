TITLE: Military Time Isn't Hard — Here's the One Rule That Makes It Click
SUBTITLE: A short, plain-language guide to the 24-hour clock, with the two exceptions (noon and midnight) that trip everyone up.

For Medium's general audience — a simple walkthrough of how military time works, where you encounter it outside the military, and why it is much easier than you think.

Most people who did not grow up around the military treat military time like a secret code. They see 1730 on a flight itinerary or a hospital wristband and assume they need to do some kind of mental math to decode it. The truth is that military time is one of the simplest systems you will ever encounter, and once you see the underlying rule, you will never need to look up 1730 again.

THE ONE RULE

The 24-hour clock runs from 0000 to 2359. Midnight is 0000, noon is 1200, and every other time is just the hour as a number, from 00 to 23, followed by the minutes.

That is it. Everything else is just padding.

For AM times, you write the hour as a two-digit number with a leading zero when needed. So 1:30 AM is 0130, and 9:45 AM is 0945.

For PM times, you add 12 to the hour. So 1:00 PM is 1300, 5:30 PM is 1730, and 11:59 PM is 2359.

THE TWO EXCEPTIONS

The two exceptions that trip people up are noon and midnight.

12:00 PM (noon) is 1200 in military time. It is not 2400. People assume noon should be 2400 because midnight feels like 0000 and noon feels like the opposite, but the system is designed so that 1200 represents noon and 2359 is one minute before the next midnight.

12:00 AM (midnight) is 0000 in military time. It is the start of the new day. 2400 is sometimes used as an alternative notation for midnight, but in standard 24-hour time, midnight is 0000.

Once you remember those two exceptions, the rest of the system is straightforward.

WHERE YOU ENCOUNTER MILITARY TIME OUTSIDE THE MILITARY

Military time is not actually a military invention. It is a global standard known as the 24-hour clock, and it is used in many places where precision matters.

Hospitals use 24-hour time because medication timing errors are dangerous, and writing 1:00 PM as 1300 removes any ambiguity about whether the dose is at 1 in the morning or 1 in the afternoon.

Airlines use it because flight schedules cross time zones constantly, and 24-hour time makes it clear exactly when a flight departs and arrives.

Emergency services use it for incident logging. International businesses use it for cross-border scheduling. And yes, the military uses it because troops operate around the clock, and there is no room for AM/PM confusion in operational planning.

WHY IT MATTERS

Most civilians will never need to convert military time in their daily lives. But the people who occasionally do — those with military family members, hospital workers, frequent flyers, and anyone who has ever stared at a 24-hour timestamp wondering what it means — will find that the system is much simpler than they thought.
