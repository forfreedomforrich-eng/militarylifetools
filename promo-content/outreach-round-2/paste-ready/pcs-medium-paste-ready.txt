TITLE: The Military PCS Timeline Nobody Tells You About (And Why Starting Early Saves You Money)
SUBTITLE: A practical guide to PCS planning, the three phases of every military move, and why the families who plan ahead have the easiest transitions.

For Medium's military community — a plain-language walkthrough of how a military PCS actually unfolds, why the pre-move phase is where most of the stress lives, and how a simple timeline changes the whole experience.

If you have ever moved military-style, you already know the feeling. You are sitting at your kitchen table with a stack of papers, a moving company on the phone, a spouse asking about the new house, and a kid asking when they will see their friends again. Everyone wants an answer, and the only thing you know for sure is that you forgot something.

That is what a PCS feels like without a plan. With a plan, it feels like a project instead of a crisis. The difference is almost entirely in the timeline.

THE THREE PHASES OF A MILITARY MOVE

Every PCS has three phases: pre-move, move week, and post-arrival. The pre-move phase is by far the longest and the most important. It usually covers 8 to 12 weeks before the move, and it is where most of the high-stakes decisions happen.

During pre-move, the service member receives orders, schedules the household goods shipment, arranges temporary lodging if needed, starts the school search for kids, begins the housing hunt at the new duty station, and gives notice on any current lease. Trying to cram all of this into the last two weeks is one of the most common PCS mistakes, and it is the single biggest source of stress during a move.

Move week is when the actual pack-out, transportation, and travel happen. It is busy, but it is mostly logistics. By the time move week arrives, the major decisions should already be made.

Post-arrival covers the first 30 to 60 days at the new duty station. It includes in-processing, setting up housing, vehicle registration, and a long list of branch-specific administrative tasks. Most of these are quick, but a few — like updating the defense enrollment eligibility reporting system — are easy to forget and important to get right.

WHY EARLY PLANNING SAVES YOU MONEY

Most service members do not realize how much money a poorly-planned PCS can cost. Rushed moves often mean booking temporary lodging at the last minute when prices are highest, accepting the first housing option they see even if it is above BAH, or paying out of pocket for expenses that would have been reimbursed with a little advance planning.

Starting the move 8 to 12 weeks early gives the service member time to compare housing options at the new duty station, coordinate with the household goods shipping office, and book temporary lodging at lower rates. It also gives the family time to prepare emotionally, which is harder to put a price on but matters just as much.

WHAT A PCS CHECKLIST ACTUALLY DOES

A PCS checklist is not just a long list of things to remember. The best ones are timeline-based, which means they organize tasks by when they need to happen, not just what they are. That makes the difference between a checklist you read once and forget, and one you actually use.

For service members who want a simple way to plan their move, a timeline-based PCS checklist is one of the most useful tools available. It does not replace the official move guidance from the transportation office, but it puts everything in one place and helps the service member stay on track.
