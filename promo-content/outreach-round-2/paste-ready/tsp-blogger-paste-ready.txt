TITLE: TSP Withdrawal Calculator 2026: How to Estimate Your Monthly Income and Taxes Before You Start Withdrawing

Long-form TSP overview for Blogger readers who want a complete picture of how Thrift Savings Plan withdrawals work, how taxes apply, and how to plan around the 4 percent rule.

The Thrift Savings Plan (TSP) is one of the most valuable retirement benefits available to federal employees and service members, but it is also one of the most underused at withdrawal time. Many retirees spend decades contributing to their TSP and then have no plan for how to actually draw it down. A TSP withdrawal calculator helps solve that problem by providing a clear estimate of how long the balance will last, what the monthly take-home will be after taxes, and how different withdrawal strategies compare.

For service members approaching retirement, this kind of estimate is one of the most useful planning exercises they can do. Without it, most people either withdraw too much too soon or too little too late.

HOW TSP WITHDRAWALS WORK

A TSP withdrawal is simply moving money out of your Thrift Savings Plan account. There are several ways to do it, including monthly installments, partial withdrawals, and a single lump-sum distribution. Each has different tax implications.

For most retirees, monthly installments are the simplest option. The TSP sends a fixed payment every month, which the retiree can treat like a pension. This works well for people who want predictable income and who do not want to manage the account themselves.

Partial withdrawals let the retiree take a specific dollar amount at a specific time. This works well for retirees who want to use TSP funds for a specific purpose, like a home purchase or a large expense.

A lump-sum distribution pays out the entire balance at once. This is rarely a good idea for tax reasons, but it can make sense in certain situations, such as when the retiree rolls the balance directly into another qualified account.

TAXES ON TSP WITHDRAWALS

TSP withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income. For traditional (pre-tax) TSP balances, every dollar withdrawn is added to the retiree's taxable income for the year. For Roth TSP balances, qualified withdrawals are tax-free.

This is why a TSP withdrawal calculator matters. A retiree who withdraws $3,000 per month from a traditional TSP balance is adding $36,000 per year to their taxable income, which can push them into a higher tax bracket, affect Social Security taxation, and increase Medicare premiums.

A calculator helps estimate that tax impact before the retiree commits to a withdrawal strategy.

WHY PLANNING EARLY MATTERS

The 4 percent rule is a popular guideline: withdraw about 4 percent of your balance in the first year of retirement, then adjust that dollar amount for inflation every year after. For a $300,000 TSP balance, that would be roughly $12,000 in the first year, or $1,000 per month.

But the 4 percent rule is just a starting point. The right withdrawal rate depends on the retiree's other income sources, expected expenses, expected lifespan, and tolerance for risk.

A TSP withdrawal calculator lets you compare different scenarios side by side. You can see how the balance lasts under different withdrawal rates, how taxes affect monthly take-home, and how long the money is likely to last under realistic assumptions.
